Understanding the Math

YTM is the internal rate of return on all future cash flows (coupons + face at maturity). It assumes you hold to maturity and reinvest coupons at the same rate. Solved via Newton-Raphson iteration.

Modified Duration tells you how much the bond price moves per 1% rate change. A duration of 3yr means the bond drops ~3% if rates rise 1%.

DV01 (Dollar Value of 01) is the $ price change per basis point (0.01%) rate move per $1,000 face value. Use it to size positions precisely.

📚 Credit Risk 101

Everything you need to understand how corporate bonds fail, who gets paid first, and why duration is the multiplier that turns a rating cut into a price crash.

⚡ The Three Types of Default

Default doesn't always mean the company went bankrupt. There are three distinct triggers, and they have very different implications for bondholders.

💳

1. Payment Default

The company misses a scheduled coupon payment or fails to repay the face value at maturity. Technically a default after a 30-day grace period written into every bond indenture.

What triggers it: Running out of cash or losing capital market access at refinancing time. The most severe form.
📋

2. Covenant Default

Violate a contractual promise in the bond indenture — even without missing a payment — and you're in technical default. Investment-grade bonds have minimal covenants. High-yield bonds have tight ones.

IG covenants (Oracle): Negative pledge, change-of-control put at 101%, cross-default clause.
HY covenants (Ford): All of the above + leverage caps, restricted payments, asset sale limits.
⛓️

3. Cross-Default

This is the domino. If a company defaults on any material debt obligation — including its bank revolving credit — it automatically triggers default on all its bonds simultaneously.

Why it matters: Banks often panic first. If Oracle's bank syndicate accelerates the revolver, every bondholder in the stack is simultaneously in default — regardless of when their bond matures.

🏛️ The Capital Stack

Every corporation has a strict hierarchy of creditors. In bankruptcy, the top layer is paid in full before the next layer sees a single dollar. Think of it as a waterfall — water only reaches lower pools when the ones above are completely full.

💧 The Bankruptcy Waterfall

If a company files Chapter 11, here's the exact order in which money flows out of the estate. Each step must be fully satisfied before the next receives anything.

1
Administrative Claims
Bankruptcy lawyers, court costs, restructuring advisors, employee retention. Can run $1-3B in a large corporate filing.
2
DIP Financing Repayment
The company needs cash to operate during bankruptcy. Existing banks usually provide "debtor-in-possession" loans at steep rates, secured by all assets, repaid at emergence.
3
Secured Creditors
Collect from their specific collateral. For Oracle, this is minimal — they have essentially no secured debt.
4
Senior Unsecured — You
Oracle's bonds + bank revolver split the remaining estate proportionally. If estate = $40B on $84B debt, recovery = ~47 cents on the dollar.
5
Equity Holders
Receive whatever remains. Usually zero. The stock is worthless by this point.

👼 The Fallen Angel Effect

The most dangerous scenario for a BBB− bondholder isn't default — it's a one-notch downgrade to BB+. Here's why that single letter change causes immediate, severe price damage even if the company is perfectly healthy.

Day 0
Rating Agencies Cut to BB+
S&P or Moody's officially downgrades Oracle from BBB− to BB+. "Junk" status. Published to all market participants simultaneously.
Day 1–3
Index Removal Announced
Bloomberg US Aggregate Bond Index, iBoxx IG index, and other IG benchmarks announce Oracle's removal at month-end rebalancing. Every fund tracking these indexes must sell.
Week 1–4
Forced Institutional Selling
~$5–7 trillion in IG-mandate funds liquidate Oracle bonds regardless of price. Insurance companies, pension funds, and bank trust departments must sell by charter. This is $40-60B in forced supply hitting the market.
Month 1–3
High-Yield Funds Absorb (Slowly)
HY funds have different mandates and price targets. They may buy, but at spreads of 500-800bps — far wider than Oracle's pre-downgrade levels. New equilibrium price forms at steep discount.
Result
Price Impact by Duration
Short bond (1yr, ModDur 0.9): drops ~2–3%. Medium bond (3yr, ModDur 2.8): drops ~6–9%. Long bond (8yr, ModDur 6.5): drops 15–22%. Same company. Same credit event. Completely different damage.

⚖️ Investment Grade vs. High Yield Covenants

The covenant package in a bond indenture is your legal protection as a creditor. IG bonds trust the company's rating and reputation. HY bonds don't — they build in contractual tripwires.

Investment Grade (Oracle, Apple, MSFT)
✓Negative Pledge — can't pledge assets as collateral without equally securing these bonds
✓Change of Control Put — you can demand 101¢ repayment if the company is acquired
✓Cross-Default — default on any material debt triggers all bonds simultaneously
✗No leverage caps or Debt/EBITDA limits
✗No restricted payment limits (Oracle can still pay dividends)
✗No asset sale limitations
High Yield (Ford, leveraged buyouts)
✓All IG covenants above, plus:
✓Incurrence Covenants — must pass a leverage test before issuing new debt or paying dividends
✓Restricted Payments Basket — limits on dividends, buybacks, and payments to equity
✓Asset Sale Sweeps — proceeds from asset sales must partially repay bonds
✓Affiliate Transaction Limits — restricts self-dealing between parent and subsidiaries
⚠ More covenants = more protection but also more complexity. A covenant breach can trigger default even when the company is cash-flow positive.
